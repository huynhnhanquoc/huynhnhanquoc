# buildinpublic

A beginner-friendly guide to sharing what you are building while you are building
it.

Build in public is not a rigid formula.

It is not a content calendar you must obey.

It is a way to let people see the journey behind a project: the idea, the messy
middle, the small wins, the wrong turns, the lessons, and the final result.

This guide is intentionally simple. Read it, take what fits, ignore what does not,
and build your own style.

## The Simple Idea

Most people only show the finished thing.

Build in public shows the path.

Instead of only saying:

```text
I launched my product.
```

You can say:

```text
I found a problem.
I tried a small solution.
It did not work the first time.
I changed the design.
Here is what I learned.
Here is what I am trying next.
```

That path is valuable.

It helps people trust you, learn from you, follow your work, and sometimes help
you make the project better.

## Why Build In Public?

You can build in public for many reasons.

- To explain what you are making.
- To find people who care about the same problem.
- To get feedback before the project is finished.
- To build trust through visible work.
- To make your learning easier to remember.
- To create proof that you can build.
- To turn your project into a story people can follow.
- To attract users, collaborators, contributors, or friends.

The deeper reason:

```text
If you do not write down the journey, the journey disappears.
```

Code can show what changed.

Public notes show why it changed.

## What Should You Share?

Share things that help people understand the project.

Good things to share:

- The problem you are trying to solve.
- Why you care about it.
- A screenshot of the current version.
- A small demo.
- A design decision.
- A technical decision.
- A mistake you learned from.
- A before-and-after comparison.
- A small milestone.
- A question you are still thinking about.
- A lesson from a user or reader.
- A roadmap for what comes next.

You do not need to share everything.

One clear thing is better than ten vague updates.

## What Should Stay Private?

Build in public is not oversharing.

Keep these private:

- passwords, tokens, credentials,
- private customer data,
- private conversations,
- legal or financial details you are not ready to disclose,
- emotional reactions you may regret,
- unverified numbers,
- security-sensitive implementation details,
- promises you are not sure you can keep.

A useful rule:

```text
Share what teaches.
Protect what can harm.
```

## Different Ways To Build In Public

There is no single correct style.

You can build in public as a maker, developer, designer, writer, founder, student,
researcher, or open-source maintainer.

Some common styles:

- **Progress log**: short updates about what changed.
- **Learning journey**: what you are studying and how your understanding changes.
- **Product story**: why the product exists, who it is for, and how it improves.
- **Technical notes**: decisions, architecture, bugs, benchmarks, implementation.
- **Design process**: sketches, iterations, visual direction, usability lessons.
- **Open-source journal**: roadmap, issues, releases, contributors, maintainership.
- **Personal story**: how the project connects to your life, taste, and curiosity.
- **Launch diary**: what happens before, during, and after launch.

Pick one main style first.

You can mix styles later.

## A 7-Day Start

If you do not know where to begin, try this.

**Day 1: Say what you are building**

Write one short post:

```text
I am building [project] for [people] because [problem].
```

**Day 2: Explain the problem**

Write why the problem matters. Keep it plain. No hype.

**Day 3: Show the first artifact**

Share a screenshot, sketch, README, demo, repo, or even a rough note.

**Day 4: Explain one decision**

Why did you choose this stack, feature, audience, or design?

**Day 5: Share one difficulty**

What is harder than you expected?

**Day 6: Ask one specific question**

Bad:

```text
Any thoughts?
```

Better:

```text
Is this landing page clear enough for someone who has never seen the product?
```

**Day 7: Write a recap**

What changed this week? What did you learn? What happens next?

After that, keep a simple rhythm:

```text
progress -> lesson -> question -> next step
```

## Where To Share

Use different places for different kinds of updates.

- **GitHub**: README, roadmap, issues, changelog, source code.
- **X/Twitter**: short updates, screenshots, quick lessons, small demos.
- **LinkedIn**: thoughtful reflections, career lessons, product lessons.
- **Personal blog**: long-form essays and permanent explanations.
- **Indie Hackers**: maker journey, product decisions, revenue, launches.
- **Product Hunt**: launches and polished product milestones.
- **Hacker News**: technical writeups with real substance.
- **Reddit**: specific discussions inside relevant communities.
- **Local communities**: native-language posts and closer conversations.

Do not paste the same post everywhere.

Adjust the message to the place.

## Communities

Communities help because building alone is hard.

Places to explore:

- build-in-public circles on X/Twitter,
- indie maker communities,
- open-source communities,
- founder communities,
- product launch communities,
- local developer groups,
- niche communities related to your product.

What to do in communities:

- Follow people who are building similar things.
- Reply with useful comments.
- Share small lessons, not only promotions.
- Ask specific questions.
- Support other builders.
- Save good examples.
- Notice what kind of posts create real discussion.

The best community habit:

```text
Give more context than you ask for.
```

## Useful Hashtags

Use hashtags lightly.

They help people find the topic, but they are not the content.

Good general hashtags:

- `#buildinpublic`
- `#buildinginpublic`
- `#shipinpublic`
- `#indiehacker`
- `#indiehackers`
- `#solomaker`
- `#indiedev`
- `#opensource`
- `#startup`
- `#saas`
- `#producthunt`

For niche projects, add one or two specific tags from your field.

Examples:

- If you build an AI app, use an AI-related tag.
- If you build a design tool, use a design-related tag.
- If you build an open-source library, use an open-source tag.
- If you build a developer tool, use a developer-tool tag.

Do not overload the post.

Two or three good hashtags are usually enough.

## Post Ideas

When you do not know what to post, pick one.

- What I am building and why.
- The problem that made me start.
- The first ugly version.
- A screenshot from today.
- A decision I made this week.
- Something I removed.
- Something I thought would be easy but was hard.
- A user question that changed my thinking.
- A mistake I made.
- A tool I am using and why.
- A before-and-after.
- A tiny win.
- A roadmap update.
- A launch lesson.
- A question I still cannot answer.

## Simple Writing Prompts

**Progress**

```text
Today I worked on [thing].
Before it worked like [old way].
Now it works like [new way].
Next I need to [next step].
```

**Decision**

```text
I chose [A] instead of [B].
The reason is [reason].
The trade-off is [cost].
I will know it worked if [signal].
```

**Failure**

```text
I tried [approach].
It failed because [reason].
I learned [lesson].
Next I will try [next approach].
```

**Learning**

```text
I used to think [old belief].
Now I think [new belief].
The thing that changed my mind was [experience].
```

**Launch**

```text
I launched [thing].
It is for [people].
It helps them [job].
The next version will focus on [next].
```

## My Build In Public

This section is intentionally small.

For my own journey, Kitwork is one project I can build in public around.

Kitwork has many ideas inside it: runtime, bytecode, virtual machine, JIT,
blueprint, routing, and other things I had to learn by building.

But this guide is not Kitwork documentation.

The story I want to preserve is simpler:

```text
I learned a concept.
I tried to build it.
Building it changed how I understood it.
Now I can explain the concept through my own experience.
```

Example:

```text
I used to think "runtime" just meant the thing that runs code.

While building Kitwork, I started seeing runtime as a boundary:
what code can access, what it cannot access, how it receives authority, and how a
request becomes a response.

That changed how I think about bytecode, VM design, and public documentation.
```

That is enough.

Kitwork can have its own separate notes.

This guide should stay focused on helping people build in public.

## Common Mistakes

- Trying to sound successful too early.
- Posting only wins.
- Posting every tiny thought.
- Copying another builder's voice.
- Turning every idea into a promise.
- Asking vague feedback.
- Using too many hashtags.
- Making the story so personal that it stops helping the reader.
- Making the guide so generic that it has no soul.

The goal is balance:

```text
useful enough to teach
honest enough to trust
personal enough to remember
```
